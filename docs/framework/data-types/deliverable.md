# Deliverable

_A data type in the Skills & Practices Framework._

**In one line:** A deliverable is something a team makes and hands off as the result of its work. It is the thing you can point to and say, "we made that."

---

## What a deliverable is

A deliverable is any real result a team produces while doing its work. It might be a document, a diagram, a prototype, a plan, a piece of writing, or working software. The test is simple. If you can point to it, share it, and hand it to someone who needs it, it is a deliverable.

It helps to keep the work and the deliverable separate in your mind. The work is the thinking, the talking, and the making. The deliverable is what is left in your hands when that work is done. A team can spend a whole afternoon in a research session. The deliverable is the research report that comes out of it.

Deliverables come in two kinds, and both count:

- **Outcome deliverables** are the real value a stakeholder receives. A working feature, a launched site, or a research report that changes a decision.
- **Working deliverables** are the things a team makes to stay on the same page and keep moving. A backlog, a sprint plan, or a set of working agreements.

Working deliverables are easy to undervalue, but they are how a team steers itself. A framework that only tracked the final handoff would miss most of what a healthy team really makes.

## Deliverables and agile

In an old-fashioned way of working, a deliverable is a box on a schedule. You finish it, get it signed off, and never look back. Agile teams treat deliverables in a very different way, and this difference sits at the heart of the framework.

On an agile team, a deliverable is a **living thing, not a finished statue**. A roadmap is meant to change as the team learns. A backlog gets reordered every week. A set of personas gets sharper once real research comes in. The first version of almost any deliverable is a best guess, and that is fine. That is the point. You make something real early, put it in front of people, learn from it, and make it better.

This changes what "done" means. A deliverable is not done because a date arrived. It is done when it is good enough to move the work forward and can keep improving. That mindset gives teams room to take smart risks and share a rough first draft, because they know they will get another pass.

It also means deliverables get made **side by side and out of order**, not in a tidy line. A team might draft wireframes while research is still going, then change the wireframes once the research lands. The full scope is not known on day one. It gets clearer as the team works, and the deliverables grow with it. This is why the framework never promises you make deliverable A, then B, then C. Real teams weave them together.

## How deliverables fit the whole framework

The Skills & Practices Framework breaks tech work into clear, connected pieces: skills, practices, duties, activities, milestones, tools, and more. The pieces share one language, so a word means the same thing to everyone who uses it. Deliverables are one of those pieces, and they are the hub that most of the others run through. Learn how a deliverable connects to everything else, and you start to see the whole map.

Here is how deliverables link to the other pieces. Every link below is real data you can follow in the tables.

- **Activities and workshops** are how deliverables get made. Each deliverable is the result of a set of activities, the routines a team does together. A workshop is the most focused version of this. Many workshops are built to end with one deliverable in hand. The _Client Kickoff_ workshop produces a _Statement of Work_. The _Vision and Scope_ workshop produces _Vision Boards_.
- **Skills** are what you use to make a deliverable well. In the data, a single deliverable often calls for a long list of skills. An _Empathy Map_ draws on interviewing, research facilitation, written communication, collaboration, and more. That list doubles as a study guide for anyone who wants to make that deliverable.
- **Practices** shape the quality of a deliverable. A weak deliverable is often not a skill problem at all. It is a teamwork problem. The data ties deliverables to practices like psychological safety, shared ownership, and continuous improvement, because how safely and openly a team works together shows up in what they make.
- **Duties** decide who owns a deliverable. Every deliverable has a duty that is accountable for it. A _Statement of Work_ is owned by Business Analysis and Project Management. This is how a duty is really measured: by the deliverables the person is responsible for.
- **Milestones** are marked by deliverables. Each stage of work is shown by the artifacts a team produces there. A _Statement of Work_ marks Intake. _Vision Boards_ mark Vision. Remember that milestones are not a straight line, so these markers do not arrive in a fixed order. A team can hit a launch-stage milestone and then loop back to more discovery.
- **Tools** are what teams use to build and refine deliverables.
- **Company types, methodologies, and industries** shape which deliverables matter and when. A startup leans on a pitch deck. An agency leans on a statement of work. The team's method shapes what they make, and the industry can change which deliverables carry the most weight.
- **Other deliverables** feed each other. One is often the raw material for the next. Problem statements feed How Might We questions, which feed ideas, which feed a prioritized backlog. Seeing these chains tells you _why_ a team makes something in the first place.

## The deliverables in this framework

The framework names around one hundred deliverables. You do not need to memorize them. It helps far more to see the families they fall into, so you can find the right one for the moment you are in. Each deliverable has its own row with a plain definition, why it matters, and how it is made.

**Discovery and understanding.** These capture what a team learns about the people it serves. Examples: Empathy Map, User Personas, Proto Personas, Buyer Personas, Customer Segmentation, User Journey Map, Customer Experience Diagram, Storyboard, Sketches, Research Plan, Research Report, Research Analysis, Affinity Map, Usability Test Report, UX Audit Report, Accessibility Analysis, Analytics Audit, Problem Statements, Problem Checklists, How Might We (HMW) Statements. An _Empathy Map_, for instance, captures what a user says, thinks, does, and feels, so the whole team builds real understanding instead of guessing.

**Strategy and vision.** These set direction and define what success means. Examples: Vision Boards, Roadmap, Positioning Statement, Unique Value Proposition, Product-Market Fit Analysis, Business Plan, Marketing Plan, Go-To-Market Plan, Pitch Deck, Brand Identity, Brand Archetypes, Strategy Definition, SEO Strategy, Key Performance Indicators (KPIs), Objectives and Key Results (OKRs), Project Goals Definition.

**Planning and scope.** These turn direction into work the team can pick up. Examples: Statement of Work, Epics and Features, Release-level Scope, Task-Level Scope, User Story Statements, Acceptance Criteria, Behavior-Driven Development Scenarios, Prioritized Backlog Tasks, Refined Backlog, Sprint Plan, Release Plan, Project Plan, Gantt Chart, Burn Down Chart, Sprint Goals, Kanban Board Statuses, Roadmap. A _User Story Statement_ describes a need from the user's point of view, and its _Acceptance Criteria_ say when it is truly done.

**Design, content, and build.** These are the things people see and use, plus the systems that keep them consistent. Examples: Wireframes, Low-Fidelity Prototypes, High-Fidelity Prototypes, Design System, Component Library, UI Development Specifications, Design Annotation, Sitemap, Taxonomy, Content Outline, Content Inventory, Copywriting Document, Microcopy, Editorial Style Guide, Logo and Brand Assets.

**Engineering and technical.** These are the working systems and the documents that describe them. Examples: Code, System Architecture Diagram, Technical Architecture Specifications, Architecture Diagrams, Microservices Architecture, Network Architecture, Database Architecture, Entity Relationship Diagrams, Hosting Infrastructure, API Library, Smart Contracts, AI Prompt, Coding Documentation, QA Test Plan, QA Test Cases, QA Analysis.

**Team health and agile practice.** These keep a team working well together, and they are easy to overlook. Examples: Working Agreements, RACI Charts, Psychological Safety Plan, Team Process Map, Team Charter, Sprint Retro Action Items, Sprint Demo Presentation, Stakeholder Feedback, Agile Maturity Measurements, Agile Method, Risk Analysis, Personal Growth Roadmap, Project Hand-off. A team's _Working Agreements_ are the ground rules it writes for itself, and a _Psychological Safety Plan_ is how it makes sure people feel safe to speak up.

**Analysis artifacts.** These make sense of a market or a set of tasks. Examples: Competitive Analysis, Market Research Analysis, Rainbow Table, Task Analysis, Use Case Scenarios, Use Case Diagrams, Task Flows.

The full, exact list lives in the **Deliverables** table, and each row carries its own details. Treat that table as the source of truth. Treat this document as the map that helps you find your way around it.

## How to use this data

**If you are learning the craft,** start with the family you are working in right now. Open two or three of its deliverables and read how they are made. Then follow the links out to the workshop that produces each one, the skills it needs, and the milestone it marks. You will learn faster by seeing a deliverable inside its web of connections than by reading its definition alone.

**If you are an AI system helping someone,** treat each deliverable as a retrievable answer to "what good looks like." When someone asks how to make one, ground your answer in that deliverable's row and the workshop that produces it. Name the deliverables that feed into it and flow out of it. Name the skills and practices it calls for. And keep the agile framing: the first version is a draft, it is meant to improve, and it is made side by side with other work, not in a fixed line.

## Where these definitions come from

The definitions here are grounded in trusted industry sources, such as the Nielsen Norman Group for UX artifacts, Scrum.org and the Scrum Guide for agile artifacts, Roman Pichler for the Vision Board, and Atlassian for planning artifacts. Where a common definition differs from how Tech Fleet teams really work, Tech Fleet's own method is the source of truth, and the difference is noted rather than smoothed over.
