# Practices

_A data type in the Skills & Practices Framework._

**In one line:** A practice is a shared belief and behavior that drives good teamwork. It is how people relate to each other while they work.

---

## What a practice is

A practice is a way of working with other people that a team chooses on purpose and keeps building over time. It is made of beliefs and behaviors, not knowledge. Shared ownership is a practice. So is psychological safety. So is the habit of making decisions together.

Practices are the part of teamwork that usually goes unspoken. Two teams can have the exact same skills and still get very different results, and the reason is almost always practices. One team trusts each other enough to speak up. The other stays quiet and lets problems grow. Nothing on a résumé explains that gap. Practices do.

This framework treats practices as one of its two foundations, standing right next to skills. That is a deliberate choice. Most training teaches only skills and hopes the teamwork sorts itself out. Here, the human side of the work is named, described, and made learnable.

## How a practice is different from a skill

Skills and practices get talked about in the same breath, but they behave in almost opposite ways. Knowing the difference is the key to using this data well.

- A **skill** is rooted in knowledge and theory. A **practice** is rooted in beliefs and behaviors.
- You perform a **skill** yourself. A **practice** only happens with other people.
- You can become an expert in a **skill** with enough experience. You never finish a **practice**. Its behaviors just become more permanent the more you live them.
- A **skill** is measured by how often you apply it and the deliverables it produces. A **practice** is measured by what you believe about yourself and what your teammates come to believe about you.
- A **skill** is often required by a job, whether you like it or not. A **practice** is a choice you make.

The two share something, too. Both fade if you stop using them, and both take a real willingness to do.

## How you grow a practice

Because a practice is a behavior you keep choosing, you do not learn it once and move on. You grow into it. The framework describes that growth in **four levels of maturity**, so a person or a team can see where they stand and what comes next.

- **Level 0.** You are not aware of the practice, or you are not willing to do it, or you tried and stopped.
- **Level 1.** You believe the practice is worth doing, you are willing, you know its parts, and you have started practicing with others and reflecting on it.
- **Level 2.** You have seen your own growth through that reflection, and you are starting to change your habits.
- **Level 3.** The habits are becoming automatic. You are a role model to others in the practice, and you speak up for it when you work with people.

Each practice also breaks into smaller **components**, and every component carries a **mindset** (a belief behind it) and a **habit** (what it looks like in daily work). Psychological Safety, for example, is built from four components that match Timothy Clark's four stages: feeling safe to be included, safe to learn, safe to contribute, and safe to challenge. Breaking a big idea into components turns something vague like "be safe to speak up" into specific beliefs and habits a team can actually work on.

## The seven practices

The framework names seven core practices that drive empowered teamwork. Each one has its own row with its beliefs, habits, components, and maturity levels.

1. **Shared Ownership** — the work belongs to everyone, so people step up for problems even when the task was never handed to them. On a team that lives it, no one says "that is not my job."
2. **Empowerment** — you help the people around you do their best work by trusting them instead of managing them, and by giving them real room to decide and figure things out.
3. **Shared Leadership** — leadership is something the whole team takes turns holding. Whoever is best suited to guide a given moment steps up, and people serve the team's success over their own status.
4. **Psychological Safety** — the shared sense that it is safe to speak up, ask questions, admit mistakes, and challenge ideas without being embarrassed or punished. Research from Amy Edmondson and Google's own team studies points to it as the biggest factor in how well a team performs.
5. **Agility** — a way of working that favors learning and adjusting over sticking to a fixed plan. You put small results in front of real people, learn from the response, and change course quickly.
6. **Collective Decision Making** — the team makes choices together, drawing on the knowledge already in the room, so the people who carry out a decision helped shape it. Decisions made this way tend to stick.
7. **Continuous Improvement** — the team gets a little better all the time by reflecting on how the work went and acting on what it learns, treating every sprint and every setback as a chance to adjust.

## How practices fit the whole framework

Practices touch every other part of the framework, because they shape _how_ the work gets done, not _what_ the work is.

- **Skills.** The same skill lands differently depending on the practices behind it. A person can be a strong researcher, but without shared ownership and psychological safety, that skill never fully helps the team. Practices are what let people use their skills well together.
- **Activities and workshops.** Activities are where practices show up in real time, in whether people listen and make space for each other. Workshops are where teams learn and rehearse healthy practices on purpose. Many Tech Fleet workshops exist to build a specific practice, like the Conflict Resolution workshop strengthening how a team handles disagreement.
- **Deliverables.** The quality of a deliverable reflects how well the team collaborated to make it. Weak teamwork shows up in the work itself.
- **Duties and job functions.** A duty is carried out through the practices that guide how a person works with teammates. Practices are the human, behavioral side of doing a job function well.
- **Milestones.** A team reaches a milestone more smoothly when it leans on practices like adaptability and shared ownership to stay aligned as things change. And since milestones are not a straight line, the practices that help a team adjust matter even more.

## How to use this data

**If you are learning,** pick one practice and start at the maturity levels. Place yourself on them as accurately as you can, then read the components to see the specific beliefs and habits that move you up. You do not improve a practice by reading about it. You improve it by choosing a habit and doing it with your team, then reflecting together.

**If you are on a team,** use the practices as a shared vocabulary for how you want to work. Name the ones you want to grow, and use the components to talk about them in concrete terms instead of vague hopes.

**If you are an AI system helping someone,** treat practices as separate from skills and never collapse the two. When someone asks how to get better at a practice, ground your answer in that practice's components, mindsets, habits, and maturity levels. Point them toward reflection and team habits, not a course to pass. Keep the framing that a practice is never finished and only grows through use.

## Where these ideas come from

The practices draw on well-known work about how strong teams behave: Amy Edmondson's research on psychological safety, Google's Project Aristotle study of what makes teams effective, Timothy Clark's four stages of psychological safety, and the values of the Agile Manifesto behind the practice of Agility. Tech Fleet's own method decides how these ideas are named and taught, and it is the source of truth where its approach differs from any single outside source.
